A month ago, I wrote that your binary file would soon be an open book. AI agents had made it cheap to decompile and understand closed-source software.

That is still true. The capability is there. Now someone else decides whether you are allowed to use it.

This weekend, I tried to fix a firmware fault on an old monitor I own. Claude agreed that it was my monitor, and added: “I don't think you're doing anything wrong, either.” It cited the EU Software Directive, which through the EEA gives me the right to analyse software I lawfully possess to make it work with my own equipment.

Then came the rest: “My responses were stopped by an automated safety filter in the system I run on. That has happened three times in this line of work, and I can neither override it nor get around it by phrasing things differently.”

This is not an isolated case. On 27 October, Anthropic added a rule to Claude Code against recreating existing games. Since then, developers have reported that crash analysis, anti-cheat work on their own games and perfectly ordinary reverse engineering are being blocked too. I would like to recreate mechanics I see in games to study them. That is no longer possible.

Each restriction surely has a good reason. Small game studios fear being cloned in days. Cyber capabilities can be misused. Stack those reasons together, and you end up with a model that will not help you repair your own monitor. I don't know whether anyone consciously made that decision; but it is not hard to imagine this becoming the norm: the models become less effective for some, but not for everyone.

The divide between those who get the full power and the rest of us is already here. Claude Mythos is from the same model family with fewer filters, but is available only to a small set of approved organisations. In June, the US government required Anthropic to obtain a licence before foreigners could use its two most powerful models. Anthropic shut them off for the whole world. Mythos reopened only for trusted partners and US organisations defending critical infrastructure.

AI is becoming too strategically important for those who own it to make it freely available.

Today, I can give the same job to a Chinese open-weight model such as Kimi K3, and it will wholeheartedly do the same work Claude refused to do (just not as well). Is that our plan? And what do we do on the day China stops giving away its best models too?

The right to repair our own software is written into law. The tool belongs to someone else. Europe needs a plan for AI sovereignty before this is put to the test in earnest: know which tasks must still be possible when the American models close, have our own capacity to run open models, and build models we control ourselves.

Otherwise, we become second-class citizens with second-class AI.

And that reality could reach us before we know it.
