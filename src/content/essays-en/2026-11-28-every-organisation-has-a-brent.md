Everyone who has worked in IT knows Brent. He is the super-engineer in The Phoenix Project, the one who can get anything done. I have met several people like him in the workplaces where I have worked. They have every access right and every authorisation, by virtue of their own ability. Brent never waits for anyone.

Now a new version is emerging: small AI-powered superteams. Blend Supply is an American family business founded in 1946 that makes custom-blended paint for aircraft and boats. Agencies used to build its online store, adding new features a few times a quarter. This summer, one developer was given a blank slate. Now agents write the requirements, the code and the code reviews, and the site gets up to 50 small improvements a day. Those are the developer's own figures, but the pattern is recognisable.

The speed does not come only from AI writing faster, but just as much from nobody having to wait. No handovers, the controls are built into the process, and the team has a mandate to change how the work is done. It is flow optimisation from Lean, only at a completely different pace.

In August, I wrote about two clock speeds: AI develops in months, organisations in years. Now we see both clocks ticking in the same company. And where they meet, conflict arises.

The superteam delivers daily. In many companies, the change advisory board meets monthly, and a security review can take weeks. The bottleneck then moves to the neighbouring department. Anthropic interviewed people at 58 companies and saw the same thing: when coding speeds up, review, sales and decisions have to keep pace, and some experts resist. That is when the leadership struggles begin: What does this mean, and who gets to decide?

The conflict is not just about speed. It is about power and fairness. The superteam gets exceptions that others do not. Roles that exist to coordinate become redundant. And the immune system kicks in: the organisation slows the team to its own pace, or the team goes around it.

The figures point in the same direction. In this year's McKinsey State of AI, 80% say AI has made them more productive. Only 37% of companies see an effect on operating profit, the same share as the year before. Perhaps that is natural. It is easier to change a team than an entire organisation.

Remember, too, what happened to Brent in the book. He was not the hero. He was the bottleneck. The knowledge was in his head, and when he was away, everything stopped.

Can AI, then, give us whole teams of Brents without the Brent problem? At Blend, everything is documented: meeting notes, work packages and why a particular solution was chosen. The knowledge is in the system, not in one person. That is what may make it possible to scale.

The question for leaders, then, is not which tool to buy. It is which clock the company should run on. Which bottlenecks genuinely protect us, and which are merely the slow clock? It is becoming increasingly clear that the whole company must change to realise the full benefit of integrated AI.

Who is Brent in your organisation? And what happens when he gets a whole team?
