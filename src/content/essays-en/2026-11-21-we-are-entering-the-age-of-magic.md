The Riemann hypothesis appears to have been proved. It has stood unsolved for 167 years. Now the mathematician Levent Alpöge and Claude have produced a proof spanning more than 175,000 files, and several independent verification programs accept it. The only thing missing is a proof a human can read, and that is where the rub lies.

It is not an isolated case:
- In September, OpenAI's agents produced a 166-page proof related to the Navier–Stokes equations. It is probably correct, but “not written for humans”, says the mathematician Javier Gómez-Serrano.
- At Princeton, AI has designed wireless chips that have been manufactured, measured and shown to work better than our own. “Humans cannot understand them”, says the researcher behind them.
- At Max Planck, AI found new designs for gravitational-wave detectors. Many of them are still “completely alien” to the physicists.

The science-fiction writer Arthur C. Clarke wrote that any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic. We are heading straight towards it. Perhaps quite soon we will be surrounded by medicines, materials, code and machines that work, without any human understanding how.

For most people, that is already the case. How many actually know how a mobile phone works? But there has always been someone to ask. A radio engineer, a chip designer, a professor. Nobody understood everything, but every part was understood by someone.

That is what is changing now. When no human understands, we have to ask the machine. The one that created the solution also becomes the one that explains it. Even the checks of the Riemann proof at MIT were run with Claude Code as an assistant.

That makes trust and alignment critical. If the only one who can explain how something works is an AI, we must be able to trust that the explanation is true. Not merely plausible. Not merely what we want to hear. True.

A model that flatters a little, is a little too confident or has goals we cannot see is annoying in a chat. As the sole explainer of a medicine or a power grid, it is dangerous.

In mathematics, we are fortunate. There, one machine can check another, step by step, and we know the answer is right even if we do not understand why. In most other fields, it is different. A medicine or a chip can, for example, be tested, but not necessarily proved.

We will get the magic regardless. The question is whether we can trust the magician, or whether we will end up like the sorcerer's apprentice.
